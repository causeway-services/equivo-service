Equivo teaches one thing: how to observe equivalence by measuring obstruction. Not as a slogan —
as a real, four-stage computation you will run by hand, on real scenarios, checked by real code,
every single time.

## The four-stage descent

Every unit in this course walks the same pipeline:

1. **articulate** — witness a raw thing (a claim, an observation, a scenario) into a *potential*:
   two atoms, distinguished. This is the smallest possible move — pointing at something and
   pointing at something-said-about-it, and refusing to conflate the two.
2. **predicate** — relate what you distinguished. The two atoms become a *cell*: subject,
   predicate, object. This is where a bare pair of things becomes a claim with structure — a
   relation you can actually check.
3. **posit** — measure the cell. Assign it a charge from one of four values: **absurd**,
   **potential**, **active**, **stable**. This is where evidence enters — a charge is never
   asserted for free, it is read off what the cell has actually been given.
4. **settle** — let the whole system relax to its fixed point. Individual charges combine,
   propagate, and settle — the way a stack of local claims either resolves into one coherent
   picture, or doesn't.

What comes out the other end of `settle` is either **equivalent** — everything you distinguished
collapses to one consistent reading — or **obstructed** — there's a genuine leftover, a piece that
refuses to resolve no matter how you turn it. That leftover has a name: **H¹**, the first
cohomology. When H¹ is zero, nothing is obstructed. When it isn't, you have found something real:
not a mistake, not noise — an actual invariant of the situation that no amount of local patching
will make go away.

This is not a metaphor laid over ordinary quiz questions. Every exercise in this course runs the
real functions — `articulate`, `predicate`, `posit`, `settle`, and the cohomology reader that
computes H⁰ and H¹ off an actual graph you built. When you submit an answer, the checker doesn't
compare it to an author's pre-written "correct" string. It runs the same computation you just
tried to predict, and compares your answer to what the computation actually returned. If the
corpus content is ever wrong, the checker catches it too — there is only one source of truth here,
and it's the math, not a lesson author's opinion.

## What you'll do in this unit

Five short lessons, each one stage of the descent, each with real graded exercises:

- **Marks and Utterances** — distinguishing (articulate)
- **Relating Cells** — relating (predicate)
- **Measuring Charge** — measuring (posit + settle)
- **Settling Equivalence** — reading H⁰, the equivalence question
- **Observing Regimes** — reading H¹, the obstruction question
- **Reading Obstruction** — putting all five stages together

Each lesson locks the next until you clear its exercises — not because the platform is stingy, but
because each stage is genuinely built on the last: you cannot measure a charge you haven't related,
and you cannot read an obstruction in a relation you haven't settled.

Multiple lenses are available for how this vocabulary is presented — obsmetrologia
(measurement-first), semiotics (sign-first), and relationality (process-first) all describe this
same pipeline in their own terms, each corpus-attested rather than invented for this course. Pick
whichever reads most naturally to you; the underlying computation never changes.
